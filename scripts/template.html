Executive summary

The short version

If you read nothing else here, read this. It sets out what the numbers below show, and what they do not.

On delivery alone, there is very little between these two governments. Labour kept 55% of the promises it could be judged on, over six years. The National-led coalition has kept 58% in two years and nine months, and has 11 promises still running that could lift it to 64%. Across 347 promises, three points apart is a tie.

What really separates them is what they promised in the first place, and the conditions they were working under.

Three-party coalition

1Labour had three times as long, and lost much of it to Covid

Six years sounds like a big advantage over two years and nine months, and it is. But Covid-19 arrived in March 2020, near the end of Labour’s first term, and it dominated the second. The border stayed shut for two and a half years. Parliament’s agenda, the Budget and most of the public service’s capacity went into the response. Promises with nothing to do with the pandemic were put off, then quietly dropped. Light rail, the rental warrant of fitness, the capital gains tax and social unemployment insurance all went this way.

This cuts both ways, and this site does not settle it. Covid is a real reason many promises were not kept. It is also true that a government is judged on the term it had, and that Labour won a larger majority in 2020, in the middle of the pandemic, on a fresh set of promises it also underdelivered. Both views are fair. What is not fair is applying the excuse to one side only.

2National has promised more, faster, and its promises were easier

195 promises in 33 months, against Labour’s 152 in 73 months. That is close to three times the rate. But the volume is not the interesting part. The composition is. 65% of National’s commitments come from signed coalition agreements, written after the election by people who already knew what could be delivered. Only 43% of Labour’s do. Across this dataset, coalition agreement commitments are kept 61% of the time and manifesto pledges 51% of the time.

The same pattern shows up in the kind of promise each party made. Promises to do a thing, like passing a law or abolishing a body, are kept 70% of the time by anyone. Promises to hit a number, like cutting hardship by so much or building so many houses, are kept 36% of the time by anyone. Put both governments on the same mix of promise types and the gap between them gets smaller, not bigger.

3National’s term is not over, and that shapes the score

Every single unfinished promise in this dataset belongs to the current government, because the other one ended in 2023. That creates a trap. Leave unfinished promises out of the score and National gets an exemption Labour cannot use. So an unfinished promise counts in the denominator, but not as a success. Labour’s incomplete long-term targets are treated exactly the same way.

Some commitments still described as under way are, by the record’s own account, moving away from their target. Jobseeker numbers are up against a pledge to cut them by 50,000. Material hardship is up against a pledge to reduce it. Those are scored as partly delivered rather than left open, which is why this section matters more than it looks.

4The real difference is what they delivered on, not how much

Both governments kept roughly three promises in five. They kept different ones. Labour delivered on trade and foreign policy, on housing rules and on climate law. It failed almost completely on transport and on the health system it had just restructured. National has delivered on law and order and on energy. It has fallen shortest on cost of living and tax, which is the ground it won the election on, and on foreign affairs and defence.

Read the area-by-area table rather than the headline. A government can keep 60% of its promises and still have kept none of the ones you voted for.

What this scorecard cannot tell you. It scores delivery, not merit. A promise kept scores the same whether the policy worked or was a disaster, and a promise broken scores the same whether dropping it was cowardice or good sense. It cannot tell you whether a government caused the changes that happened on its watch. And it comes from one round of research with its sources attached, not a peer-reviewed audit. Where a call is genuinely arguable it is marked 70 times as contested, with both readings given. Disagree with those and the number moves.

The crossover

NZ First: the same party, two governments

NZ First is the only party here to have governed in both terms. It was with Labour from 2017 to 2020, and has been with National since 2023. It is the closest thing to a controlled experiment New Zealand politics offers.

The numbers underneath

What actually moved

Promises are one thing; outcomes are another. 17 official series covering both terms. The tinted band behind each line shows who was in office. The hatched band marks Covid-19, from March 2020 to the full border reopening in August 2022. Under each chart is the change during each government’s own stretch, so you can read the two terms separately.

These are trends, not verdicts. A government inherits far more than it causes, and most of these series were already moving before it took office. Inflation and interest rates in particular were driven by a global cycle that hit every comparable country. Read the per-term changes as what happened while they were there, which is a different claim from what they did.
What is still missing, and why. Four measures are still missing: real GDP per head, average hourly earnings, police officer numbers and public housing stock. Stats NZ has retired the tables that held the first two, and its replacement site does not publish them in a readable form. Police report staff numbers only inside annual report PDFs. The housing dashboard moved when the Ministry of Housing and Urban Development became part of the new Ministry for Cities, Environment, Regions and Transport on 1 July 2026. Emissions, road deaths and rents were recovered, by opening the source agencies in a browser and reading their spreadsheets directly. Leaving a series out is honest. Estimating one is not.
How this was built

Method, and what to distrust

A scorecard is only as good as its rules. Here are the ones used, including where they are weak.

The seven outcomes

What counts as a promise

Two kinds, tagged separately throughout. Manifesto promises are what a party told voters at the election. Coalition agreement commitments are what it actually signed up to in government: the 2017 Labour and NZ First deal, the 2017 and 2020 Labour and Greens agreements, and the 2023 National agreements with ACT and with NZ First.

Where a party’s own manifesto policy was traded away in coalition talks, both are recorded and the manifesto one is marked superseded. Specific, checkable promises were preferred over aspirations.

How the score is calculated

The percentage is kept ÷ (kept + broken + in progress). Kept means delivered or largely delivered; broken means partly, not delivered, or abandoned.

An in-progress promise counts in the denominator, but not as a success. This matters more than it sounds. Every in-progress promise in the dataset belongs to the current term, so leaving them out would have given National a break Labour could not use. Promises superseded in coalition talks are left out completely, because the party never got to attempt them.

A percentage is not shown at all where fewer than 6 promises are scored. Small samples produce confident-looking numbers that mean nothing.

Three things that can skew a scorecard like this

Delivery scores are easy to tilt without meaning to. These three effects were tested for, and handled as follows.

  • Unfinished promises can only help one side. Every in-progress promise belongs to the current term, so excluding them would flatter it and nothing else. They count in the denominator instead. Commitments the record itself shows moving away from target are scored as partly delivered, not left open.
  • The kind of promise matters more than the party. Promises to do a thing are kept 70% of the time across the whole dataset. Promises to hit a number are kept 36% of the time. A party that campaigned mostly on one kind will score differently for reasons that have nothing to do with competence, so the like-for-like column reweights every party to the same mix.
  • Coalition commitments are co-signed but recorded against one party. All 19 broken signed-agreement commitments sit with ACT or NZ First, while National’s own agreement list shows no outright failure. That is a bias by role rather than by politics, and it flatters whichever party leads a government. It is not corrected for, so read partner scores with it in mind.

The risk in a scorecard like this is rarely loaded language. It is almost always the counting rules.

The comparison is not symmetrical

  • Labour governed for six years; National has governed for two years and nine months.
  • Labour’s term included Covid-19, which took over the agenda from 2020 to 2022 and killed off promises unrelated to it.
  • Labour held a single-party majority from 2020. National governs with two partners whose agreement it needs.
  • National’s term is unfinished. Some promises scored here will change status before the election.

On the Treaty section

This is the most contested material on the site, and it was handled with extra care. Policies are described by what the law actually did, in the parties’ own neutral terms.

Where the significance of a change is disputed, and it often is, both readings are set out in the promise’s contested note and the site does not pick one. Delivery is scored on whether the stated commitment happened. That is a narrower question than whether it was right.

Known weaknesses

  • Several manifesto PDFs from 2017 and 2020 could not be retrieved. Those promises cite the strongest available record of the commitment instead of the manifesto itself.
  • The Greens’ number is the least trustworthy on the site. It rests on 33 promises, 42% of them targets for things outside the party’s control, and five of them pledges in portfolios the Greens never held in Cabinet. Several cooperation agreement clauses have nothing measurable in them and are still counted. Use the like-for-like column, and treat even that with caution.
  • Treaty and Māori policy is the hardest area to score fairly and the one to read most sceptically. It carries 17 promises from the Labour years and 13 from the current term.
  • 0 promises are flagged contested, meaning the status call is genuinely arguable. Open them and judge for yourself.
  • Promise counts differ by party, so a percentage drawn from a small sample is less reliable than one drawn from a large sample. Counts are shown everywhere for that reason.
  • This is one round of research, not a peer-reviewed audit. Treat it as a starting point with its sources attached, not a final verdict.
